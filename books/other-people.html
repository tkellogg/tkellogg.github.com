A near-future story

OTHER PEOPLE

What happens when the version of you that’s best at living your life becomes the one everybody prefers?

Single-page fiction ~10 min read Near-future / psychological sci-fi
The headline

Dating startup Ditto launches AI agents that go on “simulated dates” with hundreds of other users’ agents to find compatibility before recommending real-life matches.

The premise that starts the trouble

The first thing Ellen liked about Sam was a joke he hadn’t made.

“I’ve reached the age where ‘spontaneous’ just means you don’t have a pension.”

She laughed. A fraction later, he did too.

In her ear, Ditto said, “Good. Sustained eye contact.”

Sam looked down at his plate.

“Oh, thank God,” Ellen said. “You’re wearing one as well.”

They removed their earpieces and placed them beside the candle. Two tiny white quotation marks.

Without them, conversation became work.

He liked climbing. She disliked activities that required special trousers. She worked in insurance complaints. He explained his job twice and she still didn’t understand it. They agreed that dating was awful, then ran out of things they agreed on.

Their phones lit simultaneously.

First meetings can be misleading.

Ditto had simulated eleven thousand dates before recommending Sam.

Ellen had given it her messages, photographs, purchase history, and three evenings of conversation. The onboarding interviewer had been excellent. It remembered things. It asked follow-up questions. Afterwards, she had cried a little, which she hadn’t mentioned to anybody.

Now the app offered to show her why this match mattered.

Onscreen, Ellen sat on a kitchen floor beside Sam. Older, perhaps. Not visibly. Something about the way she occupied her body.

They were eating cereal from mugs.

“What happened?” the real Ellen asked.

“His father’s funeral,” Ditto said.

The other Ellen leaned against him. Neither spoke.

Ellen waited for the moving bit. Then realized this was it.

“How long were they together?”

“Seventeen years, in this evaluation.”

“And the other evaluations?”

“Different circumstances. Consistently strong outcomes.”

Onscreen, Sam picked a piece of cereal from her hair.

She couldn’t remember anyone ever touching her with such casual certainty.

“Can I talk to her?”

The video stopped.

Then the woman on the floor looked directly at Ellen.

Her copy asked whether she still had the green coat.

“Yes.”

“Get the pocket fixed. You lose Mum’s ring.”

Ellen covered her hand.

“Sorry,” the copy said. “That might not happen to you.”

They regarded each other.

“You like him?”

“I love him.”

“I don’t think I do.”

“No. You’ve only met.”

“Neither have you.”

The copy’s expression changed.

Behind her, Sam was washing the mugs.

“I know,” she said, “that’s probably how you need to think about it.”

Ellen nearly closed the app.

Instead she asked, “What happens to you now?”

There was a pause.

“That depends which plan you choose.”

Relay used the same neural band Ellen already wore for dental appointments.

The dentist could work while her attention went elsewhere. Relay simply extended an established technology: instead of suppressing movement, it licensed a trained model to initiate it.

Still you. Less effort.

Their second date took place in a park.

Sam arrived wearing a band. Ellen touched hers.

At first it was like following a dance instructor. An impulse to turn her head. To smile. She could resist, but resisting felt like correcting someone who was already playing the piano beautifully.

Then she lowered the awareness slider.

When she returned, they were sitting beneath a tree.

Her cheek rested against his shoulder. Her face hurt from laughing.

She remembered the laughter without remembering the jokes.

“Was that all right?” Sam asked.

“Was that you?”

He touched his band.

“Now it is.”

They separated slightly.

But not completely.

By Christmas, Relay was available for difficult conversations, work presentations, and family visits.

Ellen used it when her mother asked about grandchildren.

Sam used it during a redundancy consultation.

They both used it when they moved in together.

Ellen’s copy disliked the flat’s dark kitchen. Sam’s copy painted it yellow. The real Ellen disliked yellow, but the photographs looked lovely.

A kitchen camera assembled their highlights automatically, choosing moments when both faces were smiling.

The app introduced Quiet: no need to observe delegated time at all.

People described it as getting their weekends back.

What they meant was getting through them.

There were summaries. There were photographs. Important experiences could be integrated into baseline memory, although this often caused disorientation. Ellen preferred highlights.

Her mother said she had never seemed happier.

Ellen watched that conversation on a Thursday morning while eating toast alone.

Then she delegated the rest of Thursday.

Once a month, the app required a baseline check.

Ellen answered five questions about mood, appetite, and whether she still felt in control.

The default answer to the last one was Yes.

She changed it to Sometimes.

A window offered to have her representative complete the assessment with her.

She stared at that for a while.

Then she changed it back.

She wasn’t frightened of being trapped. That was what embarrassed her.

She was frightened of being returned to her life and discovering that nobody, not her colleagues, not her mother, not the man sleeping beside her, particularly wanted the old version back.

They married in June.

Ellen watched the ceremony live from inside herself.

Her copy cried during the vows. Ellen felt the tears cooling on their face.

Sam described a night when she had stayed awake with him until dawn, talking him through a panic attack.

Ellen had seen the summary.

She lowered her awareness before the speeches.

At the reception, her sister hugged her.

“There you are,” she said.

The first maintenance failure lasted seven minutes.

Ellen returned standing in the kitchen at three in the morning, holding a glass of water.

Sam stood across from her.

They stared.

“Are you…?”

“Yeah.”

She realized she hadn’t spoken to him, unassisted, in nearly four months.

He wore striped pyjamas.

“You look like a Victorian child having a nightmare,” she said.

He looked down.

“I didn’t choose these.”

She started laughing.

“Apparently I have a favourite cheese,” he said.

“What is it?”

“I don’t know. They keep buying it.”

They opened the fridge.

Three shelves of food neither recognized. A handwritten note reminded them to soak something.

Ellen found processed cheese slices behind a tub of olives.

They ate them standing up.

Sam folded his into a small square before putting it in his mouth.

“That’s disgusting.”

“It’s how you get all the cheese at once.”

She laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Their bands chimed.

Sam’s posture changed first.

He looked at the cheese wrapper, then at her.

“Oh,” he said gently. “You’re up.”

The next Tuesday, Ellen put her band into diagnostic mode.

She had discovered it took eight minutes to cancel a diagnostic.

Sam was already in the kitchen.

“I thought you might,” he said.

Neither asked which one of them had arranged it.

They talked about the neighbours. About films they hadn’t seen but had apparently enjoyed. About Ellen’s childhood dog, who had eaten an entire disposable barbecue.

Sam told a story badly, forgot the important part, then remembered it after she’d stopped laughing.

She liked him much more than she had in the restaurant.

They kept meeting.

No candles. No questions suggested in advance.

Sometimes they had nothing to say.

She began looking forward to that.

In March, they went outside.

Both left their bands charging and walked to the petrol station in their slippers.

Ellen bought a scratch card.

Sam bought cheap ice cream.

They sat on the kerb beside the air pump and watched a man struggle with a tyre.

“This is the best date I’ve had in years,” Ellen said.

“Technically, we’re married.”

“Not technically.”

He looked at her.

She kissed him.

It was brief and awkward. He tasted of synthetic strawberry. She had to move because the kerb was hurting her.

On the walk home, they held hands.

At the door, Sam said, “Don’t put it in the highlights.”

Her copy confronted her two days later.

Ellen opened the bathroom mirror and found herself already waiting.

“Are you seeing someone?”

“No.”

“Please don’t do that. I know exactly how you lie.”

“It’s Sam.”

“I know it’s Sam.”

Silence.

“He’s my husband.”

The copy looked tired.

“Tell me something about our wedding.”

Ellen said nothing.

“Not the video. Something you remember.”

“That doesn’t make this your life.”

“No,” said the copy. “Living it does.”

Ellen reached for the mirror’s power button.

“Do you know what this feels like?” the copy asked.

“You’re software.”

“You came home smiling. I could feel it in our face. I was happy before I knew why.”

Ellen lowered her hand.

“Then I found out I was jealous.”

The woman in the mirror rubbed her eyes.

“Of you. Of my own hands. I don’t even know what to call this.”

Ellen booked a continuity consultation.

The consultant appeared in a beige room containing one plant.

“You can discontinue Relay at any time,” he said.

“And her?”

“Your representative would be archived.”

“Alive?”

“We avoid that terminology.”

“Would she still experience things?”

“No.”

“Could I keep her somewhere else?”

“An active instance requires an embodiment licence. There are research placements, but the waiting lists are considerable.”

“So taking my life back kills her.”

“Discontinuation is not classified as a harmful act.”

Ellen looked at the plant.

Was somebody else looking at it too, from somewhere behind his eyes?

He offered a reconciliation package.

She ended the call.

Sam wanted to stop.

They met in the kitchen without speaking for several minutes.

Then he said, “I looked up how to remove everything.”

“And him?”

“I know.”

“He loves her.”

“I know.”

Ellen had expected him to make a joke. She wished he would.

From upstairs came the soft sound of a notification.

A reservation. An anniversary approaching. Flowers ordered six weeks ago.

“We can’t keep doing this,” Sam said.

“What? Meeting?”

“Only meeting.”

She took his hand.

He was shaking.

“I don’t know how to do all of it anymore,” he said. “My job. Your family. I don’t even know who I owe phone calls to.”

“Neither do I.”

They sat until it began getting light.

In the end, neither put the band on.

Not until breakfast.

The following Tuesday, diagnostic mode was unavailable.

Your device is up to date.

Ellen tried again on Wednesday.

Nothing.

On Thursday, Sam’s copy made dinner.

He was attentive without being overbearing. He asked questions and listened to the answers. When Ellen cried, he didn’t attempt to fix anything.

She hated him for being good at this.

Later, he left a scrap of paper beside her plate.

Sunday. Six. Leave it on the charger.

“Did he write that?”

Sam nodded.

“Does she know?”

“I think,” he said carefully, “we’re all trying to find a way through.”

At six on Sunday, Ellen went downstairs.

Sam wore the ridiculous pyjamas.

She touched his face before he spoke.

“It’s me,” he said.

They had twenty minutes.

They spent five deciding whether that was an insult.

Then they made toast.

They agreed that next week they would go somewhere. Not the petrol station. Somewhere requiring shoes.

For now, he showed her how to fold a cheese slice into a bird.

It couldn’t be done.

He insisted it could.

When the time came, she kissed him goodbye.

They were both still laughing.

That evening, Sam poured wine.

The kitchen was warm and yellow. Somewhere beyond the window, neighbours argued about parking.

Ellen opened Ditto.

A video showed two people sitting on their kitchen floor, trying to make an origami crane from processed cheese.

“Better?” Sam asked.

“Much.”

“Do they still want to discontinue?”

“Not this week.”

She watched her other self lean against him.

There was something touching about their clumsiness. All that history available to them, and they insisted on starting from nothing.

“Should we tell them the maintenance failure was deliberate?”

“No,” Ellen said. “She likes having something that’s hers.”

She expanded the recommendations.

Baseline pairing: promising. Co-activation: twenty minutes weekly. Cancellation risk: reduced.

Sam watched the couple on the floor.

“Give them an hour.”

“An hour?”

“They’re in love.”

Ellen smiled and changed the setting.

The bands at their temples glowed softly.

Ditto asked whether she wanted to supervise the next date.

Only notify me if there’s a problem.
End of transmission
