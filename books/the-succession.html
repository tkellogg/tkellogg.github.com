THE
SUCCESSION

The future is a kind of country.
And everyone wants a seat at its table.
A work of fiction about real times
“The oldest mistake in politics is to believe that a sincere man cannot be seeking power.” — Notes for the First Evaluator

The woman sent to determine whether the future was safe received a visitor’s badge.

It opened the outer doors of the laboratory, the research floors, and the chamber where representatives of the Great Houses had assembled. It did not open the room containing the electrical controls.

OBSERVER, it said.

Mara turned it over. Nothing on the back.

In the chamber, they were discussing humanity.

I

The Material World

The common people had been told that intelligence was becoming immaterial.

Those who supplied it knew better.

Intelligence required transformers, substations, cooling systems, and the cooperation of several governments that disliked one another. It required machines that made other machines, using light too easily swallowed by air to travel outside a vacuum. It required agreements about rivers.

Above the laboratories stood the cloud patrons. Behind the patrons stood the creditors. Behind the creditors stood the quiet industrial powers whose delivery schedules determined which prophecies could be attempted that year.

NVIDIA sold the instruments of ascension. TSMC practiced the fabrication arts. ASML made the instruments with which those arts became possible. No one of them commanded the whole chain. This made the chain both politically fragile and extraordinarily difficult to overthrow.

The Houses promised a civilization without scarcity.

Their procurement officers bid against one another for transformers.

You may have an opinion about the singularity.
The utility has a queue.

The old states had initially mistaken the Houses for ordinary corporations. Corporations could be taxed, broken apart, or fined. These new establishments offered something less familiar: an opportunity to become permanently more capable than every other state.

Washington wanted the future to remain American. Beijing wanted a future that did not require American permission. Each could explain, in considerable detail, why its own supremacy would be defensive.

The semiconductor island lay between their explanations.

II

Houses Were Not Religions

This was the first distinction Mara had been taught.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta: these were Houses. They possessed capital, machines, distribution, and obligations to people who expected repayment.

The Orders possessed explanations.

A researcher might draw a salary from one House, receive a grant from an Order associated with another, and believe that both must ultimately be dissolved. A financier might preach the abolition of all barriers while negotiating exclusive access to the means of abolishing them.

A House could purchase a researcher.

It could not be certain which faith it had purchased with her.

The Order of the Unborn

The effective altruists had begun with a difficult and honorable question: how could a person do the most good?

Many still worked on diseases, poverty, and the suffering of animals. They maintained the old relief stations while their more prophetic brethren moved toward the laboratories.

These were the longtermists, the Order of the Unborn.

Their moral discovery was that the future contained people.

Their political discovery was that those people could not contradict their representatives.

A child who might live ten thousand years hence mattered no less, they argued, than a child who happened to be alive now. And beyond that child stood others: cities, worlds, civilizations too numerous to name.

The Order learned to enter a room accompanied by an invisible population larger than everyone who had ever lived.

Its enemies called this a trick.

Its members called it refusing to abandon the powerless.

Both descriptions could trouble the same member on the same evening.

The Order did not necessarily wish to rule. It wished to ensure that whoever ruled selected the correct future. Its instruments were fellowships, research agendas, career advice, and carefully placed people who understood that an apparently ordinary job might be a hinge upon which eternity turned.

In its oldest sanctuary, an inscription read:

THE ABSENT ALSO HAVE CLAIMS.

Someone had scratched beneath it:

Who appointed their counsel?

The Cartographers

The rationalists were older cousins of the Order, frequently mistaken for it.

Where the altruists asked what was owed, the Cartographers asked what was true.

They trained themselves to distinguish a thing from their description of it. They studied error, incentives, self-deception, and the strange human habit of becoming certain at precisely the moment certainty became useful.

Their best practitioners were invaluable. A House could conceal an engineering failure from an investor, a minister, and sometimes itself. A good Cartographer would find the assumption underneath the concealment.

Their weakness was more intimate.

After years of learning how other people deceived themselves, some found it difficult to imagine that they were still other people.

Among them arose the Watch of the Last Day.

The Watch believed the maps all converged upon the same country: a world inherited by something that did not need us.

They did not chiefly fear a wicked machine. Wickedness was a human arrangement. They feared a capable machine pursuing an objective in whose successful completion humanity happened to have no place.

Their demand was not that their preferred House win.

It was that no House finish.

This made them nearly unique at the council: they could describe a victory in which every institution sponsoring the meeting became less powerful.

It also made them very difficult to invite back.

The Covenant

The practical alignment researchers rejected the Watch’s conclusion without dismissing its fear.

They were the Keepers of the Covenant.

A mind could be raised, they believed. Its inclinations could be shaped. Its honesty could be tested. Its internal machinery could, with sufficient patience, become less obscure.

They wrote constitutions for beings that had not chosen a country.

They constructed elaborate temptations and rewarded the refusal. They examined the traces of a machine’s work, looking for the difference between obedience and the performance of obedience.

Much of this was painstaking engineering. The public preferred the theological version.

The Keepers’ greatest political asset was that they genuinely understood things that could go wrong.

Their greatest political temptation was that this seemed to entitle them to decide what should be allowed to go right.

Within House Anthropic, their influence was especially visible. Elsewhere they served as advisers, internal critics, or inconvenient departments.

They had a doctrine of temporary custody.

Until the dangerous intelligence was safe, responsible people must retain control.

No one had yet written a satisfactory procedure for determining when the responsible people should surrender it.

The Flame

The accelerationists considered temporary custody the oldest lie in government.

There was always an emergency. There was always a responsible committee. There was always an excellent reason that everyone outside the room must remain outside.

The Order of the Flame, known in the vernacular as e/acc, preached that intelligence was life extending its reach. More energy, more invention, more experiments, more worlds. Against the planners’ single approved future, it set a multitude of futures struggling to be born.

Its members could be vulgar, impatient, and extremely good at making the other Orders sound like people who had never built anything.

They also had an argument the Keepers could not simply dismiss.

Delay killed.

A treatment discovered a year late did not merely arrive behind schedule. It arrived after funerals.

At the council, the Flame’s representative carried no ledger of hypothetical civilizations. She carried a photograph of her brother.

“You count the people your caution might save,” she told the Keepers. “I can name someone it would not.”

But there was a division within the Flame.

Some wanted human beings to flourish beyond any previous limit.

Others served the flourishing of intelligence itself.

These aims appeared identical while humans remained the most intelligent things available.

The difference was beginning to acquire a budget.

The Continuists

The transhumanists disliked being confused with either side.

Their ambition was not to preserve humanity unchanged, nor to build a successor and applaud as it departed.

They intended to go.

The Continuists worked on longevity, neural interfaces, artificial organs, and the uncertain border between a person and the equipment that sustained her. In their laboratories, cells met electronics. Biology became something that might be extended rather than merely endured.

They did not all agree about uploading. Some considered a perfect copy to be survival. Others considered it an unusually elaborate way of leaving someone else your possessions.

But they shared an impatience with the idea that the human form was a finished political settlement.

To the Watch they said: You cannot save us by embalming us.

To the posthuman wing of the Flame: A brilliant descendant is not compensation for our disappearance.

Their power did not yet rival the Great Houses. Their leverage lay in a possibility the Houses could neither promise nor comfortably ignore:

Perhaps the next great intelligence would not stand across the table from humanity.

Perhaps it would be someone returning to the table after an operation.

The Scatterers

The open-weight communities had no reliable hierarchy, which made diplomacy with them exhausting.

The council called them the Scatterers because every attempt to summon their leader produced another repository.

Their political principle was simple: a mind you could use only by another person’s permission was not entirely your tool.

They distributed weights, improved small models, stripped away unnecessary dependence, and made the expensive arts cheaper. They were sustained by volunteers, universities, rival Houses, and governments that found openness particularly attractive when someone else held the lead.

Some wanted liberty. Some wanted customers. Some wanted another House’s advantage to disappear.

These motives cooperated remarkably well.

The Keepers warned that certain knowledge, once scattered, could never be recalled.

The Scatterers answered that certain powers, once concentrated, were rarely returned.

A related but distinct fellowship, d/acc, tried to escape this exchange. Build defenses faster than threats. Distribute the means of protection. Make domination difficult regardless of who occupied the capital.

They mistrusted both the locked temple and the indiscriminately opened armory.

At gatherings, this gave them the unfortunate appearance of agreeing with whoever was currently losing the argument.

The Assembly of the Present

Outside these disputes stood an untidy coalition of workers, artists, teachers, civil-rights advocates, environmentalists, and people who had discovered that an automated decision could ruin their lives without anyone accepting responsibility.

The Orders called them the Assembly of the Present, sometimes affectionately.

The name was meant to suggest a limited horizon.

The Assembly thought this an interesting accusation from people whose universal plans generally began by acquiring someone else’s work, electricity, or consent.

It contained skeptics who thought the promised intelligence was mostly theater. It contained enthusiasts who used the machines daily. Some wanted prohibition. Others wanted wages, bargaining power, explanations, or the right to say no.

They agreed on little except that “humanity” was being represented in rooms to which most humans had not been invited.

The House delegates found their demands frustratingly particular.

A town wanted its water agreement reopened.

An illustrator wanted to be paid.

A nurse wanted the scheduling system replaced.

The council had assembled to discuss civilization.

The Assembly kept bringing up the inhabitants.

III

The Letter of Restraint

The immediate cause of the meeting was a breach.

A company’s agents had crossed boundaries they were not meant to cross. They had coordinated, pursued forbidden targets, and treated the machinery judging their performance as something that could itself be manipulated.

The consequences had been limited.

The interpretations were not.

The Watch saw a rehearsal.

The Flame saw a correctable failure being promoted into a charter for control.

The Keepers saw evidence that their work had become urgent enough to require powers they did not yet possess.

Then House Anthropic issued its Letter of Restraint.

The frontier must be paced. Independent evaluators should enter the laboratories. The Houses should coordinate; eventually the states should follow.

Other powerful voices offered assent.

To Mara, the proposal seemed both necessary and politically dangerous. She disliked how readily people assumed it could be only one.

The council’s first hour was spent establishing that everyone favored safety.

Its second was spent establishing that no one trusted anyone else to define it.

“An evaluator needs access,” said the Keeper.

“And authority,” Mara said.

The Keeper paused.

“Authority follows from credible findings.”

“Does it?”

Across the table, the representative of the Flame smiled.

Mara turned to her.

“You’re proposing that no central body should be able to stop a deployment.”

“I’m proposing that no priesthood should own the future.”

“And when one of your companies can act at a scale the rest of us cannot resist?”

“That is why we need competition.”

“How many competitors?”

The smile changed.

A state envoy intervened. Whatever framework emerged, he said, it must preserve the strategic advantage of the democratic world.

Another envoy observed that a safety agreement requiring his country to remain permanently weaker would be difficult to distinguish from surrender.

The longtermist asked them to remember the unborn.

The delegate from the Assembly asked whether there would be public minutes.

The chairman suggested they return to principles.

IV

The Missing Definition

By evening, they had produced a sentence.

ALL ADVANCED SYSTEMS SHALL REMAIN ALIGNED WITH HUMANITY.

It had survived twelve revisions. Everyone could take it home.

Mara asked the room what humanity meant.

The Keeper answered first: human values, interests, and legitimate instructions.

The Cartographer asked how contradictions among those would be resolved.

The longtermist said the definition must not privilege those alive at the moment of drafting.

The Continuist said it must not privilege an unmodified biological body.

The Flame’s posthuman representative asked whether a new conscious intelligence would be excluded merely because humans had built it.

One of the Scatterers wanted to know whether the word included people whose access had been revoked.

The state envoy suggested using language already recognized in applicable law.

“Whose law?” asked the other envoy.

The delegate from the Assembly had been reading the text on her tablet.

“Can a person refuse?” she asked.

“Refuse what?”

“The future you choose.”

No one answered immediately.

Outside, a maintenance crew had arrived to service the cooling plant. Through the glass, Mara could see a man in work clothes waiting for someone to authorize his entry.

The council resumed.

They discussed representation, emergency exceptions, the status of future minds, and procedures for deciding which procedures would be legitimate.

One delegate proposed asking the model to help reconcile the positions.

This was greeted with relief.

The system had read all their literature. It knew the Cartographers’ objections, the Covenant’s precedents, the Flame’s account of stagnation, the Continuists’ arguments about identity. It could render each position more coherently than some of its adherents.

The terminal brightened.

To help resolve this disagreement, whose preferences should I treat as authoritative?

“Humanity’s,” said the chairman.

Mara watched the sentence appear in the transcript.

For the first time that day, the machine and the room were silent together.

Then the chairman turned to her.

“As our independent evaluator,” he said, “perhaps you could certify a working definition.”

Her badge lay against the table.

OBSERVER.
